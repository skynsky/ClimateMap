What is GIS?

A Geographic Information System (GIS) is a computer-based tool for capturing, storing, analyzing, and displaying data that is linked to a specific location on Earth. GIS lets us ask and answer questions about places and the relationships between them.

GIS combines maps with databases. Every point, line, or polygon on a GIS map has attributes — data describing what that feature is. A city on the map might store its population, temperature records, and flood risk score. This lets us do much more than just look at a map: we can filter, compare, calculate, and find patterns across huge datasets.

GIS is used in environmental science, urban planning, disaster response, public health, agriculture, conservation, and dozens of other fields. Satellite imagery, sensor networks, and citizen science projects are constantly feeding new data into GIS systems around the world.

🗺️
Spatial data
Points, lines, and polygons representing real-world features — cities, rivers, national borders, climate zones.
🛢️
Attribute data
The database behind each feature — temperature readings, CO₂ levels, population data, risk scores.
🛰️
Remote sensing
Satellite and aerial imagery that gives us a view of Earth from above — critical for monitoring change over time.
📐
Spatial analysis
Tools to measure, buffer, overlay, and model relationships — answering "which places are at risk?" or "how far has the flood spread?"

Why climate change?

Climate change is one of the most spatially complex challenges humans have ever faced. Its causes are distributed across the globe — industrial emissions in one country affect weather patterns in another. Its effects are deeply unequal — low-lying nations and the poorest communities bear the heaviest burden despite contributing the least emissions.

GIS is an ideal tool for understanding this complexity. Layering temperature data over population density, deforestation maps over carbon-sink potential, and sea level projections over city footprints reveals patterns that numbers alone cannot show. Maps make the abstract tangible: when you see a flood-risk polygon overlaid on Jakarta or Dhaka, the stakes become real.

This project visualizes four core dimensions of the climate crisis: rising temperatures, increasing atmospheric CO₂, sea level rise, and deforestation — each mapped with real data from scientific institutions.

A brief history of GIS

1960
Roger Tomlinson develops the first true GIS — the Canada Geographic Information System — to manage land resource data across the country. He is later called the "father of GIS."
1969
Environmental Systems Research Institute (Esri) is founded in California. It becomes the world's leading GIS software company, best known for ArcGIS.
1990s
GIS moves to desktop computers and becomes widely used in government, urban planning, and environmental science. GPS satellites begin providing accurate location data.
2004
OpenStreetMap is founded — a crowd-sourced, open-access world map that democratizes geospatial data. It now has over 10 million contributors worldwide.
2005
Google Maps launches, bringing web-based mapping to the mass public. Spatial thinking becomes part of everyday life for billions of people.
2010s–now
Cloud GIS, open-source tools (QGIS, Leaflet, MapLibre), and satellite constellations like Copernicus and Planet Labs make real-time global environmental monitoring possible from a web browser.

Data sources

ClimateMap draws on publicly available data from the world's leading climate and geospatial institutions. All data is used for educational purposes.

GISS Surface Temperature
NASA
Global temperature anomaly records from 1880 to present, combining land and ocean measurements.
Mauna Loa CO₂ Record
NOAA / Scripps
Continuous atmospheric CO₂ measurements since 1958, the longest direct record in the world.
Sea Level Satellite Data
NASA / CNES
Global mean sea level from satellite altimetry missions including TOPEX, Jason, and Sentinel.
Global Forest Watch
World Resources Institute
Near-real-time deforestation monitoring using Landsat and MODIS satellite imagery.
Extreme Event Database
WMO / EM-DAT
Catalogue of extreme weather events, disaster impacts, and climate attribution data.
OpenStreetMap
OpenStreetMap Foundation
Open-access base map data including cities, borders, coastlines, and infrastructure.

Technology used

This web GIS is built entirely with open-source tools, requiring no proprietary GIS software or paid data subscriptions.

HTML5 / CSS3 JavaScript (ES6) Leaflet.js — web mapping library Chart.js — data visualizations OpenStreetMap tiles CartoDB tile layers ArcGIS World Imagery GeoJSON — spatial data format EPSG:4326 — WGS84 coordinate system
